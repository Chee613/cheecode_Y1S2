# 1. Markov Model

## 1.1 What it means

A **Markov Model** is a model for sequence data where the next state depends only on the current state.

Simple meaning:

```text
Future depends only on the present.
Not the whole past.
```

Example:

```text
Today = Sunny
Tomorrow depends only on today.
```

It does not care about what happened many days ago.

```text
Past many days  ❌ ignored
Current state   ✅ used
Next state      ✅ predicted
```

---

## 1.2 Why we need it

We need Markov Models when data happens in a sequence.

Examples:

```text
Weather today → weather tomorrow
Current word character → next character
Current user action → next user action
Current market condition → next market condition
```

In Machine Learning, sequence problems are common. Markov Models give us a simple way to model how something moves from one state to another.

---

## 1.3 States

A **state** is one possible situation the system can be in.

The notes write the set of states as:

$$
{S_1, S_2, ..., S_N}
$$

Meaning:

```text
S1 = state 1
S2 = state 2
...
SN = state N
```

Example:

```text
S1 = Rain
S2 = Dry
```

So the set of states is:

$$
{Rain, Dry}
$$

Another example:

```text
S1 = rainy
S2 = cloudy
S3 = sunny
```

---

## 1.4 Transition probability

A **transition probability** is the probability of moving from one state to another state.

Formula:

$$
a_{ij}=P(S_i \mid S_j)
$$

### Formula explanation

$$
a_{ij}
$$

means:

```text
transition probability from one state to another state
```

$$
P(S_i \mid S_j)
$$

means:

```text
Probability of state Si happening,
given that the current/previous state is Sj.
```

The symbol `|` means **given**.

Example:

$$
P(Rain \mid Dry)
$$

means:

```text
Probability of Rain tomorrow,
given today is Dry.
```

It does **not** mean probability of Dry given Rain.

---

## 1.5 Initial probability

An **initial probability** tells us the probability that the sequence starts in a certain state.

Formula:

$$
\pi_i=PS_{i}
$$

Meaning:

```text
Probability that the first state is Si.
```

Example:

$$
P(Rain)=0.4
$$

means:

```text
There is 40\% chance the sequence starts with Rain.
```

Another example:

$$
P(Dry)=0.6
$$

means:

```text
There is 60\% chance the sequence starts with Dry.
```

---

# 2. Markov Chain Property

## 2.1 Formula

The Markov chain property is:

$$
P(S_{i_k} \mid S_{i_1},S_{i_2},...,S_{i_{k-1}})
=
P(S_{i_k} \mid S_{i_{k-1}})
$$

This looks difficult, but the idea is simple.

---

## 2.2 Formula explanation

The left side:

$$
P(S_{i_k} \mid S_{i_1},S_{i_2},...,S_{i_{k-1}})
$$

means:

```text
Probability of the next state,
given all previous states.
```

The right side:

$$
P(S_{i_k} \mid S_{i_{k-1}})
$$

means:

```text
Probability of the next state,
given only the immediately previous state.
```

So the full formula means:

```text
Only the previous state matters.
```

Example:

```text
Monday = Rain
Tuesday = Dry
Wednesday = Rain
Thursday = ?

Markov Model says:
Thursday depends only on Wednesday.
```

It does not use Monday and Tuesday directly.

---

## 2.3 Common mistakes

### Mistake 1: Thinking all past states are needed

Wrong:

```text
Tomorrow depends on all past days.
```

Correct:

```text
Tomorrow depends only on today.
```

### Mistake 2: Reading conditional probability backward

Wrong:

$$
P(Rain \mid Dry)
$$

means Dry given Rain.

Correct:

```text
P(Rain | Dry) means Rain given Dry.
```

---

## 2.4 Viva / exam-style questions

### Q1. What is the Markov property?

It means the next state depends only on the immediately previous state, not the full history.

### Q2. What does (P(Rain \mid Dry)) mean?

It means the probability of Rain happening next, given that the current state is Dry.

### Q3. Why is the Markov property useful?

It simplifies sequence modelling because we do not need to store or use the whole past history.

# 3. State Sequence Probability

## 3.1 What it means

State sequence probability means:

```text
Probability that a full sequence of states happens.
```

Example:

```text
Dry → Dry → Rain → Rain
```

We want to calculate:

$$
P(Dry, Dry, Rain, Rain)
$$

---

## 3.2 Formula

The formula is:

$$
P(S_{i_1},S_{i_2},...,S_{i_k})
=
P(S_{i_1})
P(S_{i_2}\mid S_{i_1})
P(S_{i_3}\mid S_{i_2})
...
P(S_{i_k}\mid S_{i_{k-1}})
$$

Simple rule:

```text
Sequence probability
=
initial probability of first state
× transition from state 1 to state 2
× transition from state 2 to state 3
× transition from state 3 to state 4
× ...
```

---

## 3.3 Example: Rain and Dry

Given:

$$
P(Rain)=0.4
$$

$$
P(Dry)=0.6
$$

$$
P(Rain\mid Rain)=0.3
$$

$$
P(Dry\mid Rain)=0.7
$$

$$
P(Rain\mid Dry)=0.2
$$

$$
P(Dry\mid Dry)=0.8
$$

Find:

$$
P(Dry, Dry, Rain, Rain)
$$

---

### Step 1: Write the sequence

```text
Dry → Dry → Rain → Rain
```

---

### Step 2: Write the formula

$$
P(Dry,Dry,Rain,Rain)
=
P(Dry)
P(Dry\mid Dry)
P(Rain\mid Dry)
P(Rain\mid Rain)
$$

---

### Step 3: Substitute values

# [

0.6 \times 0.8 \times 0.2 \times 0.3
]

---

### Step 4: Calculate

$$
0.6 \times 0.8 = 0.48
$$

$$
0.48 \times 0.2 = 0.096
$$

$$
0.096 \times 0.3 = 0.0288
$$

Final answer:

$$
P(Dry,Dry,Rain,Rain)=0.0288
$$

As percentage:

$$
0.0288 \times 100 = 2.88\%
$$

---

## 3.4 Common mistakes

### Mistake 1: Adding instead of multiplying

Wrong:

$$
0.6+0.8+0.2+0.3
$$

Correct:

$$
0.6\times0.8\times0.2\times0.3
$$

### Mistake 2: Using initial probability for every state

Wrong:

$$
P(Dry)P(Dry)P(Rain)P(Rain)
$$

Correct:

$$
P(Dry)P(Dry\mid Dry)P(Rain\mid Dry)P(Rain\mid Rain)
$$

### Mistake 3: Forgetting initial probability

If the first state is not given, include the initial probability.

### Mistake 4: Using initial probability when the first state is already given

If the question says:

```text
Day 1 is sunny.
```

Then Day 1 is already fixed, so do not multiply by (P(Sunny)).

# 4. Hidden Markov Model

## 4.1 What it means

A **Hidden Markov Model**, or **HMM**, is a Markov Model where the real state is hidden.

We cannot directly observe the hidden state.

Instead, we observe something produced by the hidden state.

Example:

```text
Hidden state: Low pressure / High pressure
Observation: Rain / Dry
```

Text diagram:

```text
Hidden state:    Low  →  High  →  High
                  ↓       ↓       ↓
Observation:    Rain     Dry     Dry
```

The hidden states are not directly visible.

The observations are visible.

---

## 4.2 Why we need HMM

We need HMM when the thing we care about is hidden.

Examples:

### Example 1: Weather

```text
Hidden state: atmospheric pressure
Observation: rain or dry
```

### Example 2: Speech recognition

```text
Hidden state: actual words or phonemes
Observation: sound signal
```

### Example 3: Word recognition

```text
Hidden state: actual character
Observation: image of the character
```

### Example 4: Character recognition

```text
Hidden state: internal character part
Observation: image slice features
```

---

## 4.3 Markov Model vs Hidden Markov Model

### Markov Model

```text
State is visible.
```

Example:

```text
Rain → Dry → Rain
```

We directly observe the state.

---

### Hidden Markov Model

```text
State is hidden.
Observation is visible.
```

Example:

```text
Hidden:       Low → High → High
Observed:    Rain   Dry    Dry
```

We only see:

```text
Rain → Dry → Dry
```

We do not directly see:

```text
Low → High → High
```

---

# 5. HMM Model Formula

An HMM is represented as:

$$
M=(A,B,\pi)
$$

Meaning:

```text
A = transition probability matrix
B = observation probability matrix
π = initial probability distribution
```

---

## 5.1 (A): Transition probability matrix

$$
A=a_{ij}
$$

$$
a_{ij}=P(S_i\mid S_j)
$$

Meaning:

```text
A stores probabilities of moving
from one hidden state to another hidden state.
```

Example:

$$
P(High\mid Low)
$$

means:

```text
Probability that the next hidden state is High,
given the current hidden state is Low.
```

So (A) is for:

```text
hidden state → hidden state
```

---

## 5.2 (B): Observation probability matrix

$$
B=(b_i(v_m))
$$

$$
b_i(v_m)=P(v_m\mid S_i)
$$

Meaning:

```text
B stores probabilities of observing visible output vm,
given hidden state Si.
```

Example:

$$
P(Rain\mid Low)
$$

means:

```text
Probability of observing Rain,
given the hidden state is Low.
```

So (B) is for:

```text
hidden state → observation
```

---

## 5.3 (\pi): Initial probability distribution

$$
\pi=(\pi_i)
$$

$$
\pi_i=PS_{i}
$$

Meaning:

```text
π stores the probability of starting in each hidden state.
```

Example:

$$
P(Low)=0.4
$$

means:

```text
There is 40\% chance the first hidden state is Low.
```

---

## 5.4 Common mistakes

### Mistake 1: Confusing (A) and (B)

Wrong:

```text
P(Rain | Low) comes from A.
```

Correct:

```text
P(Rain | Low) comes from B.
```

Because it is hidden state to observation.

---

### Mistake 2: Thinking hidden state and observation are the same

Wrong:

```text
Low = Rain
High = Dry
```

Correct:

```text
Low and High are hidden states.
Rain and Dry are observations.
```

---

# 6. Observation Sequence Probability

## 6.1 What it means

Observation sequence probability means:

```text
Probability that the HMM produces a visible observation sequence.
```

Example:

$$
O={Dry,Rain}
$$

But the hidden state sequence is unknown.

Possible hidden paths:

```text
Low  → Low
Low  → High
High → Low
High → High
```

---

## 6.2 Main formula

$$
P(O)=\sum_Q P(O,Q)
$$

Where:

```text
O = observation sequence
Q = hidden state sequence
ΣQ = sum over all possible hidden state sequences
```

Meaning:

```text
Probability of observation sequence
=
add the probability of the observation sequence
with every possible hidden path.
```

---

## 6.3 Why we add hidden paths

The same observation sequence can be produced by different hidden paths.

Example:

```text
Observation: Dry → Rain
```

Possible hidden paths:

```text
Low  → Low
Low  → High
High → Low
High → High
```

So:

$$
P(Dry,Rain)
=
P(Dry,Rain,Low,Low)
+
P(Dry,Rain,Low,High)
+
P(Dry,Rain,High,Low)
+
P(Dry,Rain,High,High)
$$

---

## 6.4 Rule: multiply inside path, add between paths

Inside one hidden path:

```text
Start state
AND observation
AND transition
AND observation
```

For **AND**, multiply.

Between hidden paths:

```text
Path 1 OR Path 2 OR Path 3 OR Path 4
```

For **OR**, add.

Simple memory:

```text
Inside one path = multiply
Between different paths = add
```

---

## 6.5 Full example: (P(Dry,Rain))

Given:

```text
Hidden states: Low, High
Observations: Rain, Dry
```

Initial probabilities:

$$
P(Low)=0.4
$$

$$
P(High)=0.6
$$

Transition probabilities:

$$
P(Low\mid Low)=0.3
$$

$$
P(High\mid Low)=0.7
$$

$$
P(Low\mid High)=0.2
$$

$$
P(High\mid High)=0.8
$$

Observation probabilities:

$$
P(Rain\mid Low)=0.6
$$

$$
P(Dry\mid Low)=0.4
$$

$$
P(Rain\mid High)=0.4
$$

$$
P(Dry\mid High)=0.6
$$

---

### Path 1: Low → Low

$$
P(Low)P(Dry\mid Low)P(Low\mid Low)P(Rain\mid Low)
$$

$$
=0.4 \times 0.4 \times 0.3 \times 0.6
$$

$$
=0.0288
$$

---

### Path 2: Low → High

$$
P(Low)P(Dry\mid Low)P(High\mid Low)P(Rain\mid High)
$$

$$
=0.4 \times 0.4 \times 0.7 \times 0.4
$$

$$
=0.0448
$$

---

### Path 3: High → Low

$$
P(High)P(Dry\mid High)P(Low\mid High)P(Rain\mid Low)
$$

$$
=0.6 \times 0.6 \times 0.2 \times 0.6
$$

$$
=0.0432
$$

---

### Path 4: High → High

$$
P(High)P(Dry\mid High)P(High\mid High)P(Rain\mid High)
$$

$$
=0.6 \times 0.6 \times 0.8 \times 0.4
$$

$$
=0.1152
$$

---

### Add all paths

$$
P(Dry,Rain)
=
0.0288+0.0448+0.0432+0.1152
$$

$$
=0.232
$$

Final answer:

$$
P(Dry,Rain)=0.232=23.2\%
$$

---

# 7. Evaluation Problem

## 7.1 What it means

The evaluation problem asks:

```text
Given HMM model M and observation sequence O,
what is the probability that M generated O?
```

Formula:

$$
P(O\mid M)
$$

Meaning:

```text
Probability of observation sequence O,
given model M.
```

Example:

```text
How likely is Dry → Rain under this HMM?
```

---

## 7.2 Why brute force is bad

If we manually consider every hidden path:

```text
N = number of hidden states
K = length of observation sequence
```

Number of possible hidden paths:

$$
N^K
$$

This grows very fast.

Example:

$$
5^{20}
$$

is extremely large.

So we use the **Forward Algorithm**.

---

# 8. Forward Recursion Algorithm

## 8.1 What it means

Forward recursion is an efficient way to calculate:

$$
P(O\mid M)
$$

Instead of listing all hidden paths manually, it stores partial probabilities.

Simple meaning:

```text
Calculate probability of reaching each hidden state
at each time step,
after seeing observations so far.
```

---

## 8.2 Forward variable

$$
\alpha_k(i)=P(o_1o_2...o_k,q_k=S_i)
$$

Meaning:

```text
αk(i)
=
probability of observing the sequence up to time k
AND being in hidden state Si at time k.
```

---

## 8.3 Step 1: Initialization

$$
\alpha_1(i)=\pi_i b_i(o_1)
$$

Meaning:

```text
Forward probability at time 1
=
initial probability of hidden state i
× probability that hidden state i produces first observation.
```

---

## 8.4 Step 2: Recursion

$$
\alpha_{k+1}(j)
=
\left[
\sum_i \alpha_k(i)a_{ij}
\right]
b_j(o_{k+1})
$$

### Formula explanation

$$
\alpha_{k+1}(j)
$$

means:

```text
Forward probability at next time step,
ending in hidden state j.
```

$$
\sum_i
$$

means:

```text
Add over all possible previous hidden states.
```

$$
\alpha_k(i)
$$

means:

```text
Probability of reaching previous hidden state i.
```

$$
a_{ij}
$$

means:

```text
Probability of moving from previous state i to current state j.
```

$$
b_j(o_{k+1})
$$

means:

```text
Probability that current state j produces current observation.
```

---

## 8.5 Step 3: Termination

$$
P(o_1o_2...o_K)=\sum_i \alpha_K(i)
$$

Meaning:

```text
At the final time step,
add all final alpha values.
```

---

## 8.6 Forward example

Observation:

$$
O={Dry,Rain}
$$

Initialization:

$$
\alpha_1(Low)=P(Low)P(Dry\mid Low)
$$

$$
=0.4 \times 0.4=0.16
$$

$$
\alpha_1(High)=P(High)P(Dry\mid High)
$$

$$
=0.6 \times 0.6=0.36
$$

---

### Recursion for (o_2=Rain)

$$
\alpha_2(Low)
=
[\alpha_1(Low)P(Low\mid Low)+\alpha_1(High)P(Low\mid High)]
P(Rain\mid Low)
$$

# [

[0.16(0.3)+0.36(0.2)](0.6)
]

# [

[0.048+0.072](0.6)
]

$$
=0.12 \times 0.6
$$

$$
=0.072
$$

---

$$
\alpha_2(High)
=
[\alpha_1(Low)P(High\mid Low)+\alpha_1(High)P(High\mid High)]
P(Rain\mid High)
$$

# [

[0.16(0.7)+0.36(0.8)](0.4)
]

# [

[0.112+0.288](0.4)
]

$$
=0.4 \times 0.4
$$

$$
=0.16
$$

---

### Termination

$$
P(Dry,Rain)=\alpha_2(Low)+\alpha_2(High)
$$

$$
=0.072+0.16
$$

$$
=0.232
$$

Final answer:

$$
P(Dry,Rain)=0.232=23.2\%
$$

---

## 8.7 Common mistakes

### Mistake 1: Forgetting observation probability

Wrong:

$$
\alpha_2(Low)=0.16(0.3)+0.36(0.2)
$$

Correct:

$$
\alpha_2(Low)=[0.16(0.3)+0.36(0.2)]P(Rain\mid Low)
$$

---

### Mistake 2: Forgetting final summation

Wrong:

```text
Answer = alpha of one final state only
```

Correct:

$$
P(O)=\sum_i\alpha_K(i)
$$

---

### Mistake 3: Using max instead of summation

Forward algorithm uses:

$$
\sum
$$

not:

$$
\max
$$

# 9. Decoding Problem

## 9.1 What it means

The decoding problem asks:

```text
Given HMM model M and observation sequence O,
what is the most likely hidden state sequence?
```

Example:

```text
Observation: Dry → Rain
Question: Which hidden path most likely produced it?
```

Possible hidden paths:

```text
Low  → Low
Low  → High
High → Low
High → High
```

---

## 9.2 Evaluation vs decoding

| Problem    | Question                       | Output                     |
| ---------- | ------------------------------ | -------------------------- |
| Evaluation | How likely is the observation? | $P(O\mid M)$               |
| Decoding   | What hidden path caused it?    | Best hidden state sequence |

Simple memory:

```text
Evaluation = probability of observation
Decoding = hidden explanation
```

---

# 10. Viterbi Algorithm

## 10.1 What it means

The **Viterbi Algorithm** solves the decoding problem.

It finds the most likely hidden state path.

Main idea:

```text
Forward algorithm adds all paths.
Viterbi keeps only the best path.
```

---

## 10.2 Viterbi variable

$$
\delta_k(i)
$$

Meaning:

```text
Best path probability up to time k,
ending in hidden state Si.
```

---

## 10.3 Step 1: Initialization

$$
\delta_1(i)=\pi_i b_i(o_1)
$$

Same idea as forward initialization.

---

## 10.4 Step 2: Recursion

$$
\delta_k(j)
=
\left[
\max_i \delta_{k-1}(i)a_{ij}
\right]
b_j(o_k)
$$

### Formula explanation

$$
\max_i
$$

means:

```text
Try every previous state i,
but keep only the largest probability.
```

$$
\delta_{k-1}(i)
$$

means:

```text
Best previous path probability ending in state i.
```

$$
a_{ij}
$$

means:

```text
Transition probability from previous state i to current state j.
```

$$
b_j(o_k)
$$

means:

```text
Observation probability of current observation from current state j.
```

So the formula means:

```text
Best path to current state
=
best previous path
× transition
× current observation probability
```

---

## 10.5 Step 3: Termination

$$
\max_i \delta_K(i)
$$

Meaning:

```text
At the final time step,
choose the hidden state with the largest delta value.
```

---

## 10.6 Step 4: Backtracking

Backtracking means:

```text
Trace backward from the best final state
to recover the full best hidden path.
```

Example:

```text
Best final state = High
Best previous state before High = High

Best path = High → High
```

---

## 10.7 Viterbi example

Observation:

$$
O={Dry,Rain}
$$

Initialization:

$$
\delta_1(Low)=0.4 \times 0.4=0.16
$$

$$
\delta_1(High)=0.6 \times 0.6=0.36
$$

---

### Calculate (\delta_2(Low))

$$
\delta_2(Low)
=
\max[0.16(0.3),0.36(0.2)](0.6)
$$

# [

\max[0.048,0.072](0.6)
]

$$
=0.072 \times 0.6
$$

$$
=0.0432
$$

Best previous state:

```text
High
```

---

### Calculate (\delta_2(High))

$$
\delta_2(High)
=
\max[0.16(0.7),0.36(0.8)](0.4)
$$

# [

\max[0.112,0.288](0.4)
]

$$
=0.288 \times 0.4
$$

$$
=0.1152
$$

Best previous state:

```text
High
```

---

### Termination

$$
\max[0.0432,0.1152]=0.1152
$$

Best final state:

```text
High
```

Backtracking:

```text
High → High
```

Final answer:

$$
\text{Best hidden path}=High \rightarrow High
$$

$$
\text{Best path probability}=0.1152
$$

---

## 10.8 Common mistakes

### Mistake 1: Using summation in Viterbi

Wrong:

$$
\sum_i \delta_{k-1}(i)a_{ij}
$$

Correct:

$$
\max_i \delta_{k-1}(i)a_{ij}
$$

---

### Mistake 2: Thinking Viterbi gives (P(O))

Wrong:

```text
Viterbi result = total observation probability
```

Correct:

```text
Viterbi result = best hidden path probability
```

---

### Mistake 3: Forgetting backtracking

Viterbi must give the full path, not only the final state.

# 11. Learning Problem

## 11.1 What it means

The learning problem asks:

```text
Given observation sequences,
how do we learn the best HMM parameters A, B, and π?
```

In the earlier problems, (A), (B), and (\pi) were already given.

But in real life, we may not know these probabilities.

So the model must learn them from training data.

---

## 11.2 Goal

$$
M^*=\arg\max_M P(O\mid M)
$$

Meaning:

```text
Find the HMM model M
that makes the observed training data O most likely.
```

Where:

$$
M=(A,B,\pi)
$$

So learning means finding good values for:

```text
A = transition probabilities
B = observation probabilities
π = initial probabilities
```

---

## 11.3 Why learning is difficult

If hidden states are known, we can count.

Example:

```text
Hidden states: Low → Low → High → High
Observations:  Dry   Rain  Rain   Dry
```

Then we can count:

```text
Low → High
High → High
Low produces Rain
High produces Dry
```

But in real HMM learning, hidden states are unknown:

```text
Observations:  Dry → Rain → Rain → Dry
Hidden states: ?      ?      ?      ?
```

So we cannot directly count hidden transitions.

That is why we need special algorithms.

---

## 11.4 Baum-Welch / EM idea

The common learning algorithm is **Baum-Welch**, which is based on **Expectation-Maximization**, or EM.

Simple version:

```text
1. Start with guessed A, B, and π.
2. Estimate likely hidden state behavior.
3. Update A, B, and π.
4. Repeat until the model stops improving.
```

Text diagram:

```text
Guess A, B, π
      ↓
Estimate hidden states
      ↓
Update A, B, π
      ↓
Repeat
```

---

## 11.5 Local maximum idea

Baum-Welch does not always guarantee the global best model.

It may find a **local maximum**.

Simple meaning:

```text
The model improves from its starting point,
but it may not find the best possible parameters overall.
```

This is similar to the local minima / global minima idea from gradient descent, except here we are maximizing probability.

# 12. Word Recognition with HMM

## 12.1 Why this section matters

The lecture includes word recognition examples using HMM.

This is important because it shows HMM is not only for weather.

HMM can be used in real applications such as:

```text
word recognition
character recognition
speech recognition
handwriting recognition
sequence labelling
```

---

## 12.2 Big idea

In word recognition, the computer sees an image of a word.

Example:

```text
Image: A m h e r s t
```

But the computer does not directly know the real letters.

So HMM treats:

```text
Hidden states = actual characters
Observations = character images
```

Text diagram:

```text
Hidden character:     A       m       h       e       r       s       t
                      ↓       ↓       ↓       ↓       ↓       ↓       ↓
Observed image:     img1    img2    img3    img4    img5    img6    img7
```

The hidden state is the true character.

The observation is the image shape seen by the computer.

---

## 12.3 Character recognizer score

The lecture uses:

$$
P(image \mid character)
$$

Meaning:

```text
Probability of seeing this image,
given that the real character is a certain letter.
```

Example:

$$
P(image\mid a)=0.5
$$

means:

```text
If the real character is "a",
there is 0.5 probability that it produces this image.
```

Another example:

$$
P(image\mid b)=0.03
$$

means:

```text
If the real character is "b",
there is only 0.03 probability that it produces this image.
```

If the image looks like “a”, then:

$$
P(image\mid a)
$$

should be high.

---

## 12.4 Important: this belongs to (B)

Recall:

$$
B = P(observation \mid hidden\ state)
$$

For word recognition:

$$
B = P(image \mid character)
$$

So:

```text
Hidden state = character
Observation = image
Observation probability = P(image | character)
```

# 13. Word Recognition with a Lexicon

## 13.1 What is a lexicon?

A **lexicon** means a fixed list of possible words.

Example:

```text
Possible words:
Amherst
Buffalo
```

---

## 13.2 Separate HMM for each word

If a lexicon is given, we can build one HMM per word.

Example:

```text
HMM for Amherst
HMM for Buffalo
```

Then we calculate:

$$
P(O\mid M_{Amherst})
$$

and:

$$
P(O\mid M_{Buffalo})
$$

where:

```text
O = observed word image sequence
M = word HMM model
```

Then choose the word with the larger probability.

---

## 13.3 Text diagram

```text
Input word image
       ↓
Evaluate HMM for "Amherst" → probability score
Evaluate HMM for "Buffalo" → probability score
       ↓
Choose word with larger probability
```

---

## 13.4 What HMM problem is this?

This is the **Evaluation Problem**.

Why?

Because we ask:

$$
P(O\mid M)
$$

Meaning:

```text
How likely is this observed image sequence
under this word model?
```

If:

$$
P(O\mid M_{Amherst}) > P(O\mid M_{Buffalo})
$$

then choose:

```text
Amherst
```

# 14. Word Recognition with One Big HMM

## 14.1 One HMM for all words

Another method is to build one big HMM for all words.

In this model:

```text
Hidden states = all alphabet characters
```

Example:

```text
a, b, c, d, ..., z
```

The observations are still:

```text
images of characters
```

---

## 14.2 What question are we asking?

Now we ask:

```text
Which sequence of letters most likely produced this image?
```

Example:

```text
Observed images: img1 img2 img3
Possible hidden letters: c a t
```

So the model tries to find:

```text
Best hidden character sequence
```

---

## 14.3 What HMM problem is this?

This is the **Decoding Problem**.

Why?

Because decoding means:

```text
Find the most likely hidden state sequence.
```

Here:

```text
Hidden state sequence = character sequence
```

So:

```text
Lexicon with separate word HMMs = Evaluation problem
Single HMM over alphabet = Decoding problem
```

## 16.1 Exercise idea

The lecture exercise asks:

```text
Observed sequence = {1, 3, 2, 1}

Which HMM is more likely to generate this observation sequence:
HMM for "A" or HMM for "B"?
```

This is another **Evaluation Problem**.

We calculate:

$$
P(O\mid M_A)
$$

and:

$$
P(O\mid M_B)
$$

Then compare.

---

## 16.2 Given result

The slide gives:

$$
P(O\mid M_A)=0.0023976
$$

$$
P(O\mid M_B)=0.0096768
$$

Since:

$$
0.0096768 > 0.0023976
$$

the HMM for **B** is more likely to generate the observed sequence.

So the recognized character is:

$$
\boxed{B}
$$

---

## 16.3 Why do we add multiple paths?

The same observation sequence:

$$
{1,3,2,1}
$$

can be generated by different hidden state paths.

Example paths:

```text
s1 → s1 → s2 → s3
s1 → s2 → s2 → s3
s1 → s2 → s3 → s3
```

So:

$$
P(O\mid M_A)
=
P(O,\text{path 1})
+
P(O,\text{path 2})
+
P(O,\text{path 3})
$$

Same for HMM (M_B).

This follows the same rule:

```text
Inside one path = multiply
Between possible paths = add
```

---

## 16.4 One path probability pattern

For one hidden path:

$$
P(start)
\times
P(observation_1\mid state_1)
\times
P(state_2\mid state_1)
\times
P(observation_2\mid state_2)
\times
...
$$

Simple version:

```text
initial probability
× observation probability
× transition probability
× observation probability
× transition probability
× ...
```

## 16.5 Common mistakes in word / character recognition

### Mistake 1: Thinking the image is the hidden state

Wrong:

```text
Hidden state = image
```

Correct:

```text
Hidden state = real character
Observation = image of the character
```

---

### Mistake 2: Confusing (P(image\mid character)) with (P(character\mid image))

The lecture uses:

$$
P(image\mid character)
$$

This means:

```text
Probability of seeing the image,
given the character.
```

It does not directly mean:

```text
Probability that the character is "a",
given the image.
```

---

### Mistake 3: Thinking lexicon method is decoding

If we build one HMM per word and compare probabilities, that is:

```text
Evaluation problem
```

If we build one HMM over all letters and find the best hidden letter sequence, that is:

```text
Decoding problem
```

---

### Mistake 4: Forgetting to add hidden paths

For a given character HMM, many hidden state paths can produce the same observation sequence.

So we must add them:

$$
P(O)=\sum_Q P(O,Q)
$$

# 19. Staying in a State for Exactly (d) Days

## 19.1 Formula

$$
P_i(d)=(a_{ii})^{d-1}(1-a_{ii})
$$

---

## 19.2 Meaning of (a_{ii})

$$
a_{ii}
$$

means:

```text
Probability of staying in the same state i.
```

Example:

$$
a_{33}=P(S_3\rightarrow S_3)=0.8
$$

Meaning:

```text
If today is sunny,
probability tomorrow is still sunny is 0.8.
```

---

## 19.3 Why (d-1)?

Suppose we start sunny on Day 1 and stay sunny for exactly 3 days.

```text
Day 1: sunny
Day 2: sunny
Day 3: sunny
Day 4: not sunny
```

Stay transitions:

```text
Day 1 → Day 2
Day 2 → Day 3
```

There are 2 stay transitions.

Since:

$$
d=3
$$

then:

$$
d-1=2
$$

So the formula uses:

$$
(a_{ii})^{d-1}
$$

Then the model must leave the state:

$$
1-a_{ii}
$$

---

## 19.4 Example: sunny exactly 3 days

Sunny state:

$$
S_3
$$

Self-transition:

$$
a_{33}=0.8
$$

Exact duration:

$$
d=3
$$

Formula:

$$
P_3(3)=(a_{33})^{3-1}(1-a_{33})
$$

Substitute:

$$
P_3(3)=(0.8)^2(1-0.8)
$$

$$
=(0.64)(0.2)
$$

$$
=0.128
$$

Final answer:

$$
P_3(3)=0.128=12.8\%
$$

# 21. Python Code Examples

## 21.1 Markov sequence probability

```python
states = {
    "rainy": 0,
    "cloudy": 1,
    "sunny": 2
}

A = [
    [0.4, 0.3, 0.3],
    [0.2, 0.6, 0.2],
    [0.1, 0.1, 0.8]
]

sequence = [
    "sunny",
    "sunny",
    "sunny",
    "rainy",
    "rainy",
    "sunny",
    "cloudy",
    "sunny"
]

probability = 1

for i in range(len(sequence) - 1):
    current_state = sequence[i]
    next_state = sequence[i + 1]

    current_index = states[current_state]
    next_index = states[next_state]

    transition_probability = A[current_index][next_index]
    probability *= transition_probability

print(probability)
print(probability * 100, "%")
```

Output:

```text
0.0001536
0.01536 %
```

## 21.2 Forward algorithm

```python
hidden_states = ["Low", "High"]

initial = {
    "Low": 0.4,
    "High": 0.6
}

transition = {
    "Low": {
        "Low": 0.3,
        "High": 0.7
    },
    "High": {
        "Low": 0.2,
        "High": 0.8
    }
}

emission = {
    "Low": {
        "Rain": 0.6,
        "Dry": 0.4
    },
    "High": {
        "Rain": 0.4,
        "Dry": 0.6
    }
}

observations = ["Dry", "Rain"]

alpha = {}

for state in hidden_states:
    alpha[state] = initial[state] * emission[state][observations[0]]

for t in range(1, len(observations)):
    current_observation = observations[t]
    new_alpha = {}

    for current_state in hidden_states:
        total = 0

        for previous_state in hidden_states:
            total += alpha[previous_state] * transition[previous_state][current_state]

        new_alpha[current_state] = total * emission[current_state][current_observation]

    alpha = new_alpha

probability = sum(alpha.values())

print(probability)
```

Output:

```text
0.232
```

## 21.3 Viterbi algorithm

```python
hidden_states = ["Low", "High"]

initial = {
    "Low": 0.4,
    "High": 0.6
}

transition = {
    "Low": {
        "Low": 0.3,
        "High": 0.7
    },
    "High": {
        "Low": 0.2,
        "High": 0.8
    }
}

emission = {
    "Low": {
        "Rain": 0.6,
        "Dry": 0.4
    },
    "High": {
        "Rain": 0.4,
        "Dry": 0.6
    }
}

observations = ["Dry", "Rain"]

delta = {}
path = {}

for state in hidden_states:
    delta[state] = initial[state] * emission[state][observations[0]]
    path[state] = [state]

for t in range(1, len(observations)):
    current_observation = observations[t]
    new_delta = {}
    new_path = {}

    for current_state in hidden_states:
        best_probability = -1
        best_previous_state = None

        for previous_state in hidden_states:
            probability = (
                delta[previous_state]
                * transition[previous_state][current_state]
                * emission[current_state][current_observation]
            )

            if probability > best_probability:
                best_probability = probability
                best_previous_state = previous_state

        new_delta[current_state] = best_probability
        new_path[current_state] = path[best_previous_state] + [current_state]

    delta = new_delta
    path = new_path

best_final_state = max(delta, key=delta.get)

print("Best path:", path[best_final_state])
print("Probability:", delta[best_final_state])
```

Output:

```text
Best path: ['High', 'High']
Probability: 0.1152
```

# 23. Final Common Mistakes

1. Reading conditional probability backward.
2. Adding transition probabilities instead of multiplying.
3. Forgetting initial probability when the first state is not given.
4. Using initial probability when the first state is already given.
5. Confusing hidden state with observation.
6. Confusing (A) and (B).
7. Using (\max) in Forward Algorithm.
8. Using (\sum) in Viterbi Algorithm.
9. Thinking Viterbi gives (P(O)).
10. Forgetting backtracking in Viterbi.
11. Thinking word images are hidden states.
12. Confusing (P(image\mid character)) with (P(character\mid image)).
13. Using (a_{ii}^d) instead of (a_{ii}^{d-1}).
14. Using (\frac{1}{a_{ii}}) instead of (\frac{1}{1-a_{ii}}).

# 24. Viva / Exam Questions

## Q1. What is a Markov Model?

A Markov Model is a model where the next state depends only on the current or previous state, not the whole past.

---

## Q2. What is the Markov property?

$$
P(S_{i_k}\mid S_{i_1},...,S_{i_{k-1}})
=
P(S_{i_k}\mid S_{i_{k-1}})
$$

It means the next state depends only on the immediately previous state.

---

## Q3. What is an HMM?

A Hidden Markov Model is a model where the real states are hidden, but each hidden state can produce visible observations.

---

## Q4. What are (A), (B), and (\pi)?

```text
A = transition probability matrix
B = observation probability matrix
π = initial probability distribution
```

---

## Q5. What is the evaluation problem?

Given (M=(A,B,\pi)) and observation sequence (O), find:

$$
P(O\mid M)
$$

---

## Q6. What algorithm solves the evaluation problem?

The Forward Algorithm.

---

## Q7. What is the decoding problem?

Given (M=(A,B,\pi)) and observation sequence (O), find the most likely hidden state sequence.

---

## Q8. What algorithm solves the decoding problem?

The Viterbi Algorithm.

---

## Q9. What is the main difference between Forward and Viterbi?

```text
Forward uses summation to add all paths.
Viterbi uses maximum to choose the best path.
```

---

## Q10. What is the learning problem?

The learning problem finds the best (A), (B), and (\pi) from training observation sequences.

---

## Q11. What algorithm is used for HMM learning?

Baum-Welch algorithm, based on Expectation-Maximization.

---

## Q12. In word recognition HMM, what are hidden states?
