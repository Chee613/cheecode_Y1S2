# 1. Markov Model

## 1.1 What it means

A **Markov Model** is a model for sequence data where the next state depends only on the current state.

Simple meaning:

```text
Future depends only on the present.
Not the whole past.
```

Example:

```text
Today = Sunny
Tomorrow depends only on today.
```

The lecture defines a Markov Model using states, transition probabilities, and initial probabilities. It also states the Markov chain property, where the next state depends only on the previous state. 

---

## 1.2 Set of states

The lecture writes the set of states as:

$$
{S_1, S_2, ..., S_N}
$$

Meaning:

```text
S1 = state 1
S2 = state 2
...
SN = state N
```

Example:

```text
S1 = Rain
S2 = Dry
```

So the state set is:

$$
{Rain, Dry}
$$

---

## 1.3 Transition probability

Transition probability means the probability of moving from one state to another state.

Formula:

$$
a_{ij}=P(S_i \mid S_j)
$$

Meaning:

```text
Probability of going to state Si,
given that the current state is Sj.
```

Example:

$$
P(Rain \mid Dry)
$$

means:

```text
Probability of Rain tomorrow,
given today is Dry.
```

Common mistake:

```text
P(Rain | Dry) does NOT mean probability of Dry given Rain.
The part after | is the condition.
```

---

## 1.4 Initial probability

Initial probability means the probability that the model starts in a state.

Formula:

$$
\pi_i=P(S_i)
$$

Meaning:

```text
Probability that the first state is Si.
```

Example:

$$
P(Rain)=0.4
$$

means:

```text
There is 40% chance the sequence starts with Rain.
```

---

# 2. Markov Chain Property

The lecture gives:

$$
P(S_{i_k} \mid S_{i_1},S_{i_2},...,S_{i_{k-1}})
P(S_{i_k} \mid S_{i_{k-1}})
$$

## Explanation

The left side:

$$
P(S_{i_k} \mid S_{i_1},S_{i_2},...,S_{i_{k-1}})
$$

means:

```text
Probability of the next state,
given all previous states.
```

The right side:

$$
P(S_{i_k} \mid S_{i_{k-1}})
$$

means:

```text
Probability of the next state,
given only the previous state.
```

So the full formula means:

```text
Only the previous state matters.
```

# 3. State Sequence Probability

## 3.1 Formula

The lecture gives the state sequence probability formula as:

$$
P(S_{i_1},S_{i_2},...,S_{i_k})
P(S_{i_k}\mid S_{i_{k-1}})
P(S_{i_{k-1}}\mid S_{i_{k-2}})
...
P(S_{i_2}\mid S_{i_1})
P(S_{i_1})
$$

The lecture uses this to calculate the probability of a state sequence such as Dry, Dry, Rain, Rain. 

---

## 3.2 Simple rule

```text
Sequence probability
=
initial probability of first state
× transition probability from state 1 to state 2
× transition probability from state 2 to state 3
× ...
```

---

## 3.3 Example

Given:

$$
P(Rain)=0.4
$$

$$
P(Dry)=0.6
$$

$$
P(Rain\mid Rain)=0.3
$$

$$
P(Dry\mid Rain)=0.7
$$

$$
P(Rain\mid Dry)=0.2
$$

$$
P(Dry\mid Dry)=0.8
$$

Find:

$$
P(Dry, Dry, Rain, Rain)
$$

Formula:

$$
P(Dry)
\times
P(Dry\mid Dry)
\times
P(Rain\mid Dry)
\times
P(Rain\mid Rain)
$$

Substitute:

$$
=0.6 \times 0.8 \times 0.2 \times 0.3
$$

Calculate:

$$
0.6 \times 0.8 = 0.48
$$

$$
0.48 \times 0.2 = 0.096
$$

$$
0.096 \times 0.3 = 0.0288
$$

Final answer:

$$
P(Dry,Dry,Rain,Rain)=0.0288
$$

As percentage:

$$
0.0288 \times 100 = 2.88%
$$

# 4. Hidden Markov Model

## 4.1 What it means

A **Hidden Markov Model**, or **HMM**, is a Markov Model where the real state is hidden.

We cannot directly observe the hidden state.

Instead, we observe something produced by the hidden state.

Example:

```text
Hidden state: Low pressure / High pressure
Observation: Rain / Dry
```

Text diagram:

```text
Hidden state:    Low  →  High  →  High
                  ↓       ↓       ↓
Observation:    Rain     Dry     Dry
```

The lecture states that HMM states are not visible, but each hidden state randomly generates observations or visible states. 

---

## 4.2 Difference between Markov Model and HMM

### Markov Model

```text
State is visible.
```

Example:

```text
Rain → Dry → Rain
```

We directly observe the states.

---

### Hidden Markov Model

```text
State is hidden.
Observation is visible.
```

Example:

```text
Hidden:       Low → High → High
Observed:    Rain   Dry    Dry
```

We only see:

```text
Rain → Dry → Dry
```

We do not directly see:

```text
Low → High → High
```

---

# 5. HMM Model Formula

The lecture represents an HMM as:

$$
M=(A,B,\pi)
$$

The tutorial also asks you to describe the elements of (M=(A,B,\pi)). 

---

## 5.1 (A): Transition probability matrix

$$
A=(a_{ij})
$$

$$
a_{ij}=P(S_i\mid S_j)
$$

Meaning:

```text
A stores probabilities of moving from one hidden state to another hidden state.
```

Example:

$$
P(High\mid Low)
$$

means:

```text
Probability that the next hidden state is High,
given the current hidden state is Low.
```

---

## 5.2 (B): Observation probability matrix

$$
B=(b_i(v_m))
$$

$$
b_i(v_m)=P(v_m\mid S_i)
$$

Meaning:

```text
B stores probabilities of observing visible output vm,
given hidden state Si.
```

Example:

$$
P(Rain\mid Low)
$$

means:

```text
Probability of observing Rain,
given the hidden state is Low.
```

---

## 5.3 (\pi): Initial probability distribution

$$
\pi=(\pi_i)
$$

$$
\pi_i=P(S_i)
$$

Meaning:

```text
π stores the probability of starting in each hidden state.
```

Example:

$$
P(Low)=0.4
$$

means:

```text
There is 40% chance the first hidden state is Low.
```

The tutorial handwritten solution describes (A), (B), and (\pi) as transition, observation, and initial probability components of HMM.

# 6. Observation Sequence Probability

## 6.1 What it means

Observation sequence probability means:

```text
Probability that the HMM produces a visible observation sequence.
```

Example:

$$
O={Dry,Rain}
$$

But the hidden state sequence is unknown.

Possible hidden paths:

```text
Low  → Low
Low  → High
High → Low
High → High
```

---

## 6.2 Main formula

$$
P(O)=\sum_Q P(O,Q)
$$

Where:

```text
O = observation sequence
Q = hidden state sequence
ΣQ = sum over all possible hidden state sequences
```

Meaning:

```text
Probability of observation sequence
=
add the probability of the observation sequence with every possible hidden path.
```

---

## 6.3 Why add all hidden paths?

Because the same observation sequence can be produced by many possible hidden state paths.

Example:

```text
Observation: Dry → Rain
```

could come from:

```text
Low  → Low
Low  → High
High → Low
High → High
```

So:

$$
P(Dry,Rain)
P(Dry,Rain,Low,Low)
+
P(Dry,Rain,Low,High)
+
P(Dry,Rain,High,Low)
+
P(Dry,Rain,High,High)
$$

---

## 6.4 Multiply inside one path, add between paths

Inside one hidden path:

```text
Start state
AND observation
AND transition
AND observation
```

For **AND**, multiply.

Between hidden paths:

```text
Path 1 OR Path 2 OR Path 3 OR Path 4
```

For **OR**, add.

Simple memory:

```text
Inside one path = multiply
Between different paths = add
```

# 7. Evaluation Problem

## 7.1 What it means

The evaluation problem asks:

```text
Given HMM model M and observation sequence O,
what is the probability that M generated O?
```

Formula:

$$
P(O\mid M)
$$

The lecture defines the evaluation problem as calculating the probability that model (M=(A,B,\pi)) generated an observation sequence (O=o_1o_2...o_K). 

---

## 7.2 Why brute force is bad

If we manually consider every hidden path:

```text
N = number of hidden states
K = length of observation sequence
```

Number of hidden paths:

$$
N^K
$$

This grows very fast.

Example:

$$
5^{20}
$$

is extremely large.

So we use the **Forward Algorithm**.

---

# 8. Forward Recursion Algorithm

## 8.1 What it means

Forward recursion is an efficient way to calculate:

$$
P(O\mid M)
$$

Instead of listing all hidden paths manually, it stores partial probabilities.

The tutorial says forward recursion calculates the probability of an observation sequence by computing the probability of reaching each state at each time step while considering previous possible paths. 

---

## 8.2 Forward variable

$$
\alpha_k(i)=P(o_1o_2...o_k,q_k=S_i)
$$

Meaning:

```text
αk(i)
=
probability of observing the sequence up to time k
AND being in hidden state Si at time k.
```

---

## 8.3 Step 1: Initialization

$$
\alpha_1(i)=\pi_i b_i(o_1)
$$

Meaning:

```text
Forward probability at time 1
=
initial probability of hidden state i
× probability that hidden state i produces first observation.
```

---

## 8.4 Step 2: Recursion

$$
\alpha_{k+1}(j)
\left[
\sum_i \alpha_k(i)a_{ij}
\right]
b_j(o_{k+1})
$$

Meaning:

```text
To calculate alpha for current state j:

1. Look at every possible previous state i.
2. Take previous alpha value αk(i).
3. Multiply by transition probability aij.
4. Add all possible previous paths.
5. Multiply by observation probability bj(ok+1).
```

---

## 8.5 Step 3: Termination

$$
P(o_1o_2...o_K)=\sum_i \alpha_K(i)
$$

Meaning:

```text
At the final time step,
add all final alpha values.
```

---

## 8.6 Forward example

Observation:

$$
O={Dry,Rain}
$$

Hidden states:

```text
Low, High
```

Initial:

$$
P(Low)=0.4
$$

$$
P(High)=0.6
$$

Observation probabilities:

$$
P(Dry\mid Low)=0.4
$$

$$
P(Dry\mid High)=0.6
$$

$$
P(Rain\mid Low)=0.6
$$

$$
P(Rain\mid High)=0.4
$$

Transition probabilities:

$$
P(Low\mid Low)=0.3
$$

$$
P(High\mid Low)=0.7
$$

$$
P(Low\mid High)=0.2
$$

$$
P(High\mid High)=0.8
$$

---

### Initialization

$$
\alpha_1(Low)=P(Low)P(Dry\mid Low)
$$

$$
=0.4 \times 0.4=0.16
$$

$$
\alpha_1(High)=P(High)P(Dry\mid High)
$$

$$
=0.6 \times 0.6=0.36
$$

---

### Recursion for (o_2=Rain)

$$
\alpha_2(Low)
[\alpha_1(Low)P(Low\mid Low)+\alpha_1(High)P(Low\mid High)]
P(Rain\mid Low)
$$

# [

[0.16(0.3)+0.36(0.2)](0.6)
]

# [

[0.048+0.072](0.6)
]

$$
=0.12 \times 0.6
$$

$$
=0.072
$$

---

$$
\alpha_2(High)
[\alpha_1(Low)P(High\mid Low)+\alpha_1(High)P(High\mid High)]
P(Rain\mid High)
$$

# [

[0.16(0.7)+0.36(0.8)](0.4)
]

# [

[0.112+0.288](0.4)
]

$$
=0.4 \times 0.4
$$

$$
=0.16
$$

---

### Termination

$$
P(Dry,Rain)=\alpha_2(Low)+\alpha_2(High)
$$

$$
=0.072+0.16
$$

$$
=0.232
$$

Final answer:

$$
P(Dry,Rain)=0.232=23.2%
$$

# 9. Decoding Problem

## 9.1 What it means

The decoding problem asks:

```text
Given HMM model M and observation sequence O,
what is the most likely hidden state sequence?
```

The lecture lists decoding as one of the main HMM issues: given (M=(A,B,\pi)) and observation sequence (O), calculate the most likely hidden states that produced (O). 

---

## 9.2 Evaluation vs Decoding

| Problem    | Question                       | Output                     |
| ---------- | ------------------------------ | -------------------------- |
| Evaluation | How likely is the observation? | $(P(O\mid M))$               |
| Decoding   | What hidden path caused it?    | Best hidden state sequence |

Simple memory:

```text
Evaluation = probability of observation
Decoding = hidden explanation
```

---

# 10. Viterbi Algorithm

## 10.1 What it means

The **Viterbi Algorithm** solves the decoding problem.

It finds the most likely hidden state path.

Main idea:

```text
Forward algorithm adds all paths.
Viterbi keeps only the best path.
```

---

## 10.2 Viterbi variable

$$
\delta_k(i)
$$

Meaning:

```text
Best path probability up to time k,
ending in hidden state Si.
```

---

## 10.3 Step 1: Initialization

$$
\delta_1(i)=\pi_i b_i(o_1)
$$

Same as forward initialization.

---

## 10.4 Step 2: Recursion

$$
\delta_k(j)
\left[
\max_i \delta_{k-1}(i)a_{ij}
\right]
b_j(o_k)
$$

Meaning:

```text
To calculate best path ending at current state j:

1. Look at every previous state i.
2. Take best previous probability δk-1(i).
3. Multiply by transition probability aij.
4. Choose the maximum.
5. Multiply by current observation probability bj(ok).
```

---

## 10.5 Step 3: Termination

$$
\max_i \delta_K(i)
$$

Meaning:

```text
At the final time step,
choose the final state with the largest delta value.
```

---

## 10.6 Step 4: Backtracking

Backtracking means:

```text
Trace backward from the best final state
to recover the full best hidden path.
```

---

## 10.7 Viterbi example

Observation:

$$
O={Dry,Rain}
$$

Initialization:

$$
\delta_1(Low)=0.4 \times 0.4=0.16
$$

$$
\delta_1(High)=0.6 \times 0.6=0.36
$$

---

### Calculate (\delta_2(Low))

$$
\delta_2(Low)
\max[0.16(0.3),0.36(0.2)](0.6)
$$

# [

\max[0.048,0.072](0.6)
]

$$
=0.072 \times 0.6
$$

$$
=0.0432
$$

Best previous state:

```text
High
```

---

### Calculate (\delta_2(High))

$$
\delta_2(High)
\max[0.16(0.7),0.36(0.8)](0.4)
$$

# [

\max[0.112,0.288](0.4)
]

$$
=0.288 \times 0.4
$$

$$
=0.1152
$$

Best previous state:

```text
High
```

---

### Termination

$$
\max[0.0432,0.1152]=0.1152
$$

Best final state:

```text
High
```

Backtracking:

```text
High → High
```

Final answer:

$$
\text{Best hidden path}=High \rightarrow High
$$

$$
\text{Best path probability}=0.1152
$$

# 11. Learning Problem

## 11.1 What it means

The learning problem asks:

```text
Given observation sequences,
how do we learn the best HMM parameters A, B, and π?
```

The lecture lists learning as one of the main HMM issues: given training observation sequences and the general HMM structure, determine (M=(A,B,\pi)) that best fits the training data. 

---

## 11.2 Goal

$$
M^*=\arg\max_M P(O\mid M)
$$

Meaning:

```text
Find the HMM model M
that makes the observed training data O most likely.
```

Where:

$$
M=(A,B,\pi)
$$

So learning means finding good values for:

```text
A = transition probabilities
B = observation probabilities
π = initial probabilities
```

---

## 11.3 Why learning is difficult

If hidden states are known, we can count.

Example:

```text
Hidden states: Low → Low → High → High
Observations:  Dry   Rain  Rain   Dry
```

Then we can count:

```text
Low → High
High → High
Low produces Rain
High produces Dry
```

But in real HMM learning, hidden states are unknown:

```text
Observations:  Dry → Rain → Rain → Dry
Hidden states: ?      ?      ?      ?
```

So we cannot directly count hidden transitions.

That is why we need special algorithms such as **Baum-Welch**, which is based on **Expectation-Maximization**.

---

## 11.4 Baum-Welch idea

Simple version:

```text
1. Start with guessed A, B, and π.
2. Estimate likely hidden state behavior.
3. Update A, B, and π.
4. Repeat until the model stops improving.
```

Text diagram:

```text
Guess A, B, π
      ↓
Estimate hidden states
      ↓
Update A, B, π
      ↓
Repeat
```

# 13. Staying in a State for Exactly (d) Days

## 13.1 Formula

$$
P_i(d)=(a_{ii})^{d-1}(1-a_{ii})
$$

The tutorial asks for the probability of staying in state (i) for exactly (d) days and the expected duration. The handwritten solution uses this formula and the expected duration formula. 

---

## 13.2 Meaning of (a_{ii})

$$
a_{ii}
$$

means:

```text
Probability of staying in the same state i.
```

Example:

$$
a_{33}=P(S_3\rightarrow S_3)=0.8
$$

Meaning:

```text
If today is sunny,
probability tomorrow is still sunny is 0.8.
```

---

## 13.3 Why (d-1)?

Suppose we start sunny on Day 1 and stay sunny for exactly 3 days.

```text
Day 1: sunny
Day 2: sunny
Day 3: sunny
Day 4: not sunny
```

Stay transitions:

```text
Day 1 → Day 2
Day 2 → Day 3
```

There are 2 stay transitions.

Since:

$$
d=3
$$

then:

$$
d-1=2
$$

So the formula uses:

$$
(a_{ii})^{d-1}
$$

Then the model must leave the state:

$$
1-a_{ii}
$$

---

## 13.4 Example: sunny exactly 3 days

Sunny state:

$$
S_3
$$

Self-transition:

$$
a_{33}=0.8
$$

Exact duration:

$$
d=3
$$

Formula:

$$
P_3(3)=(a_{33})^{3-1}(1-a_{33})
$$

Substitute:

$$
P_3(3)=(0.8)^2(1-0.8)
$$

$$
=(0.64)(0.2)
$$

$$
=0.128
$$

Final answer:

$$
P_3(3)=0.128=12.8%
$$

# 14. Expected Duration in a State

## 14.1 Formula

$$
E(d)=\frac{1}{1-a_{ii}}
$$

Meaning:

```text
Expected duration
=
average number of days the model stays in state i.
```

---

## 14.2 Why (1-a_{ii})?

$$
a_{ii}
$$

means probability of staying.

So:

$$
1-a_{ii}
$$

means probability of leaving.

If leaving probability is small, the state lasts longer.

# 16. Python Code Examples

## 16.1 Markov sequence probability

```python
states = {
    "rainy": 0,
    "cloudy": 1,
    "sunny": 2
}

A = [
    [0.4, 0.3, 0.3],
    [0.2, 0.6, 0.2],
    [0.1, 0.1, 0.8]
]

sequence = [
    "sunny",
    "sunny",
    "sunny",
    "rainy",
    "rainy",
    "sunny",
    "cloudy",
    "sunny"
]

probability = 1

for i in range(len(sequence) - 1):
    current_state = sequence[i]
    next_state = sequence[i + 1]

    current_index = states[current_state]
    next_index = states[next_state]

    transition_probability = A[current_index][next_index]
    probability *= transition_probability

print(probability)
print(probability * 100, "%")
```

Output:

```text
0.0001536
0.01536 %
```

hidden_states = ["Low", "High"]

    },
    "High": {
        "Low": 0.2,
        "High": 0.8
    }
}

emission = {
    "Low": {
        "Rain": 0.6,
        "Dry": 0.4
    },
    "High": {
        "Rain": 0.4,
        "Dry": 0.6
    }
}

observations = ["Dry", "Rain"]

alpha = {}

for state in hidden_states:
    alpha[state] = initial[state] * emission[state][observations[0]]

for t in range(1, len(observations)):
    current_observation = observations[t]
    new_alpha = {}

    for current_state in hidden_states:
        total = 0

        for previous_state in hidden_states:
            total += alpha[previous_state] * transition[previous_state][current_state]

        new_alpha[current_state] = total * emission[current_state][current_observation]

    alpha = new_alpha

probability = sum(alpha.values())

print(probability)

## 16.3 Viterbi algorithm

```python
hidden_states = ["Low", "High"]

initial = {
    "Low": 0.4,
    "High": 0.6
}

transition = {
    "Low": {
        "Low": 0.3,
        "High": 0.7
    },
    "High": {
        "Low": 0.2,
        "High": 0.8
    }
}

emission = {
    "Low": {
        "Rain": 0.6,
        "Dry": 0.4
    },
    "High": {
        "Rain": 0.4,
        "Dry": 0.6
    }
}

observations = ["Dry", "Rain"]

delta = {}
path = {}

for state in hidden_states:
    delta[state] = initial[state] * emission[state][observations[0]]
    path[state] = [state]

for t in range(1, len(observations)):
    current_observation = observations[t]
    new_delta = {}
    new_path = {}

    for current_state in hidden_states:
        best_probability = -1
        best_previous_state = None

        for previous_state in hidden_states:
            probability = (
                delta[previous_state]
                * transition[previous_state][current_state]
                * emission[current_state][current_observation]
            )

            if probability > best_probability:
                best_probability = probability
                best_previous_state = previous_state

        new_delta[current_state] = best_probability
        new_path[current_state] = path[best_previous_state] + [current_state]

    delta = new_delta
    path = new_path

best_final_state = max(delta, key=delta.get)

print("Best path:", path[best_final_state])
print("Probability:", delta[best_final_state])
```

Output:

```text
Best path: ['High', 'High']
Probability: 0.1152
```

# 17. Final Formula Sheet

## Markov property

$$
P(S_{i_k}\mid S_{i_1},...,S_{i_{k-1}})
P(S_{i_k}\mid S_{i_{k-1}})
$$

## State sequence probability

$$
P(S_{i_1},...,S_{i_k})
P(S_{i_1})
P(S_{i_2}\mid S_{i_1})
...
P(S_{i_k}\mid S_{i_{k-1}})
$$

## HMM model

$$
M=(A,B,\pi)
$$

---

## Transition probability

$$
a_{ij}=P(S_i\mid S_j)
$$

---

## Observation probability

$$
b_i(v_m)=P(v_m\mid S_i)
$$

---

## Initial probability

$$
\pi_i=P(S_i)
$$

---

## Observation sequence probability

$$
P(O)=\sum_Q P(O,Q)
$$

---

## Forward initialization

$$
\alpha_1(i)=\pi_i b_i(o_1)
$$

---

## Forward recursion

$$
\alpha_{k+1}(j)
\left[
\sum_i \alpha_k(i)a_{ij}
\right]
b_j(o_{k+1})
$$

---

## Forward termination

$$
P(O)=\sum_i \alpha_K(i)
$$

---

## Viterbi initialization

$$
\delta_1(i)=\pi_i b_i(o_1)
$$

---

## Viterbi recursion

$$
\delta_k(j)
\left[
\max_i \delta_{k-1}(i)a_{ij}
\right]
b_j(o_k)
$$

$$
P_i(d)=(a_{ii})^{d-1}(1-a_{ii})

$$

# 18. Common Mistakes

1. Reading conditional probability backward.

Wrong:

$$
P(Rain\mid Dry)
$$

means Dry given Rain.

Correct:

```text
It means Rain given Dry.
```

---

2. Adding transition probabilities instead of multiplying.

Wrong:

$$
0.8+0.8+0.1
$$

Correct:

$$
0.8 \times 0.8 \times 0.1
$$

---

3. Using initial probability when the first state is already given.

If the question says:

```text
Day 1 is sunny.
```

Do not multiply by (P$(S_3)$).

---

4. Forgetting initial probability when the first state is not given.

If the first state is not given, include:

$$
P(S_{first})
$$

---

5. Confusing hidden state and observation.

```text
Hidden state = not directly visible
Observation = visible output
```

---

6. Confusing (A) and (B).

```text
A = hidden state → hidden state
B = hidden state → observation
```

---

7. Using summation in Viterbi.

Wrong:

$$
\sum_i
$$

Correct:

$$
\max_i
$$

---

8. Thinking Viterbi gives (P(O)).

Wrong:

```text
Viterbi result = total observation probability
```

Correct:

```text
Viterbi result = best hidden path probability
```

---

9. Forgetting backtracking in Viterbi.

Viterbi needs the full hidden path, not just the final largest value.

---

10. Using (a_{ii}^d) instead of (a_{ii}^{d-1}).

Correct:

$$
P_i(d)=(a_{ii})^{d-1}(1-a_{ii})
$$

---

11. Using (\frac{1}{a_{ii}}) instead of (\frac{1}{1-a_{ii}}).

Correct:

$$
E(d)=\frac{1}{1-a_{ii}}
$$

# 19. Viva / Exam Questions

## Q1. What is a Markov Model?

A Markov Model is a model where the next state depends only on the current or previous state, not the whole past.

---

## Q2. What is the Markov property?

$$
P(S_{i_k}\mid S_{i_1},...,S_{i_{k-1}})
P(S_{i_k}\mid S_{i_{k-1}})
$$

It means the next state depends only on the immediately previous state.

---

## Q3. What is an HMM?

A Hidden Markov Model is a model where the real states are hidden, but each hidden state can produce visible observations.

---

## Q4. What are (A), (B), and (\pi)?

```text
A = transition probability matrix
B = observation probability matrix
π = initial probability distribution
```

---

## Q5. What is the evaluation problem?

Given (M=(A,B,\pi)) and observation sequence (O), find:

$$
P(O\mid M)
$$

---

## Q6. What algorithm solves the evaluation problem?

The Forward Algorithm.

---

## Q7. What is the decoding problem?

Given (M=(A,B,\pi)) and observation sequence (O), find the most likely hidden state sequence.

---

## Q8. What algorithm solves the decoding problem?

The Viterbi Algorithm.

---

## Q9. What is the main difference between Forward and Viterbi?

```text
Forward uses summation to add all paths.
Viterbi uses maximum to choose the best path.
```

---

## Q10. What is the learning problem?

The learning problem finds the best (A), (B), and (\pi) from training observation sequences.

---

## Q11. What algorithm is used for HMM learning?

Baum-Welch algorithm, based on Expectation-Maximization.
